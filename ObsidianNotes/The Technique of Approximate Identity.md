The idea is to introduce a family of kernel functions $\{K_{\epsilon}\}_{\epsilon>0}$ approximating the identity transformation via convolution. For a given function $f$, we expect $f_{\epsilon}:=K_{\epsilon}*f$ has great properties and converge to $f$ in some sense as ${\epsilon}\to0^+$. Besides, many operations on $f$, such as derivatives, can be transferred onto $K_{\epsilon}$, on which they may behave much better.
### A. A General Version
**Definition.** $\{K_{\epsilon}\}_{\epsilon>0}$ is a family of kernels if
1. $\int_{\mathbb{R}^n}K_{\epsilon}(x)\,dx=1$
2. $\sup_{\epsilon>0}||K_{\epsilon}||_1<\infty$
3. $\forall\delta>0\;\lim_{\epsilon\to0^+}\int_{|x|\geq\delta}|K_{\epsilon}(x)|\,dx=0$
Also we have a stronger condition
4. $|K_{\epsilon}(x)|\leq A{\epsilon}^{-n}$ and $|K_{\epsilon}(x)|\leq\frac{A\epsilon}{||x||^{n+1}},\,\forall x\neq 0$
Notice that 4 implies 2&3. Often we take a mother function to generate kernels.
**Lemma.** Given $K\in L^1(\mathbb{R}^n)$ such that $\int_{\mathbb{R}^n}K(x)\,dx$, denote $K_{\epsilon}(x)=K(x/\epsilon)/{\epsilon}^n$. Then $\{K_{\epsilon}\}_{\epsilon>0}$ satisfies condition 1-3. If furthermore, $K$ is bounded by $A$ and $|K(x)|\leq \frac{A}{||x||^{n+1}},\,\forall x\neq0$, then the stronger condition is satisfied.

Recall the definition of average function$$
(A_rf)(x):=\frac{1}{\mu(B(x,r))}\int_{B(x,r)}f(y)\,dy
$$the Hardy-Littlewood Maximal function$$
(Mf)(x):=\sup_{r>0}\frac{1}{\mu(B(x,r))}\int_{B(x,r)}|f(y)|\,dy
$$and the definition of Lebesgue point$$
L_f:=\Big\{x\in\mathbb{R}^n:\frac{1}{\mu(B(x,r))}\int_{B(x,r)}|f(y)-f(x)|\,dy\to 0\text{ as }r\to0^+\Big\}
$$**Lemma.** Let $f\in L^1(\mathbb{R}^n)$, $x\in L_f$, and let$$
A_x(r)=\frac{1}{r^n}\int_{|y|\leq r}|f(x-y)-f(x)|\,dy,\;r>0.
$$Then $A_x(r)$ is bounded and continuous. And $A_x(r)\to0\text{ as }r\to0^+$.
**Proof.** Continuity of $A_x(r)$ follows from the absolute continuity of Lebesgue integral. By the choice of $x$ is clear that $A_x(r)\to0\text{ as }r\to0^+$. Also$$
A_x(r)\leq\frac{1}{r^n}\int_{|y|\leq r}|f(x-y)|\,dy+\frac{1}{r^n}\int_{|y|\leq r}|f(x)|\,dx\leq r^{-n}||f||_1+\mu(B(1))|f(x)|
$$So it's bounded.$$\tag*{$\square$}$$**Theorem.** Let $\{K_{\epsilon}\}_{\epsilon>0}$ be a family of kernels and denote $f_{\epsilon}:=K_{\epsilon}*f$.
1. If $f$ satisfies [average continuity](On%20Average%20Continuity%20and%20Smooth%20Approximation.md), say $f\in L^p(\mathbb{R}^n)$ where $p\in[1,\infty)$, or $f\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^n)$ and is uniformly continuous, then $f_{\epsilon}\to f\text{ in }L^p$, or $f_{\epsilon}\rightrightarrows f\text{ on }\mathbb{R}^n$ (i.e., $f_{\epsilon}\to f\text{ in }L^{\infty}$).
2. If $f\in L^1(\mathbb{R}^n)$ and furthermore, $\{K_{\epsilon}\}_{\epsilon>0}$ satisfies the stronger condition, then $f_{\epsilon}\to f\;\; p.w.\text{ on }L_f$. Since $L_f^c$ is a null set, $f_{\epsilon}\to f\;\;a.e.$.
**Proof.** **Case 1.** $$
\begin{align}
||f_{\epsilon}-f||_p&=\big|\big|\,||K_{\epsilon}(y)[f(x-y)-f(x)]||_{L^1_y}\,\big|\big|_{L^p_x}\\
&\leq\big|\big|\,||K_{\epsilon}(y)[f(x-y)-f(x)]||_{L^p_x}\,\big|\big|_{L^1_y}\\
&=\int_{\mathbb{R}^n}|K_{\epsilon}(y)|\cdot||f(x-y)-f(x)||_{L^p_x}\,dy
\end{align}
$$By average continuity, $\forall\delta>0\,\exists r>0\,\forall |y|<r\;\;||f(x-y)-f(x)||_{L^p_x}<\delta$. So$$
\begin{align}
\int_{\mathbb{R}^n}|K_{\epsilon}(y)|\cdot||f(x-y)-f(x)||_{L^p_x}\,dy&\leq\delta+2||f||_p\int_{|y|\geq r}|K_{\epsilon}(y)|\,dy\\
\end{align}
$$Hence $$
\limsup_{\epsilon\to0^+}||f_{\epsilon}-f||_p\leq\delta
$$and hence $f_{\epsilon}\to f\text{ in }L^p$.
**Case 2.** For any $\delta>0$ and $x\in L_f$,$$
\begin{align}
|f_{\epsilon}(x)-f(x)|&\leq\Big(\int_{|y|<\delta}+\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\int_{2^{k}\delta<|y|\leq2^{k+1}\delta}\Big)|f(x-y)-f(x)|\cdot |K_{\epsilon}(y)|\,dy\\
&\leq \frac{\delta^nA}{\epsilon^n}A_x(\delta)+\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{A\epsilon}{(2^k\delta)^{n+1}}A_x(2^{k+1}\delta)
\end{align}
$$Let $M$ be a boundary of $A_x$. For any $\eta>0$, take $N$ such that $\sum_{k\geq N}2^{-k}<\eta$. Take $\delta=\epsilon$ small enough, so that $A_x(2^k\delta)<\frac{\eta}{N}$, for all $k<N$. Then we have$$
|f_{\epsilon}(x)-f(x)|<C\eta
$$$$\tag*{$\square$}$$**Remark.** Notice this powerful technique called "dyadic decomposition"!
### B. As a Way to Lebesgue's Differentiation Theorem
$$
\begin{align}
A_rf(x)&=\frac{1}{m(B(x,r))}\int_{B(x,r)}f(y)\,dy\\
&=\int_{\mathbb{R}^n}f(y)\frac{\chi_{B(r)}(x-y)}{\mu(B(r))}\,dy\\
&=K_r*f(x)
\end{align}
$$$\{K_r\}_{r>0}$ is generated by $K(x)=\frac{\chi_B(1)}{\mu(B(1))}$. Clearly $K$ satisfies the corresponding stronger condition. Now in dimension 1, $A_rf$ is closely related to differentials.
### C. Mollifier
**Definition.** A standard mollifier $\eta$ on $\mathbb{R}^n$ is a function such that
1. $\eta\in C_c^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^n)$
2. $\int_{\mathbb{R}^n}\eta(x)\,dx=1$
A classic construction is$$
\eta(x)=\left\{
\begin{aligned}
&c\cdot \exp{\frac{1}{|x|^2-1}}&\text{if }|x|<1,\\
&0&\text{if }|x|\geq1.
\end{aligned}
\right.
$$
Where $c$ is chosen to guarantee that the integral is 1. Similarly, we construct a family of convolution kernels $\{\eta_{\epsilon}\}_{\epsilon>0}$. We see that this construction satisfies
3. $\eta_{\epsilon}(x)=h_{\epsilon}(|x|)$ for some $h$ (spherically symmetric)
4. $\text{supp}\,\eta_{\epsilon}\subset\bar{B}(\epsilon)$

**Proposition.** For any $f\in L^1_{loc}(\mathbb{R}^n)$, $f_{\epsilon}:=\eta_{\epsilon}*f\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^n)$. Furthermore,
1. if $f\in L^p(\mathbb{R}^n)$ where $p\in[1,\infty)$, or $f\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^n)$ then $f_{\epsilon}\to f\text{ in }L^p\text{ as }\epsilon\to0^+$, and
2. if $f\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^n)$ and $f$ is uniformly continuous on $\mathbb{R}^n$, then $f_{\epsilon}\rightrightarrows f$ on $\mathbb{R}^n$.
**Proof.** A direct corollary of the general version.$$\tag*{$\square$}$$**Theorem.** For $p\in [1,\infty)$, $C_c^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^n)$ is dense in $L^p(\mathbb{R}^n)$.
**Proof.** Trivial by the proposition above.$$\tag*{$\square$}$$
### D. Gaussian Kernel
**Definition.** The Gaussian Kernel function is $G(x):=e^{-\pi|x|^2}$.
**Lemma.** $\widehat{G}=G$
**Proof.** This can be proved either by solving and ODE or by direct calculating via Cauchy's Integral Formula in complex analysis.$$\tag*{$\square$}$$**Corollary.** $\widehat{G_{\epsilon}}(\xi)=\widehat{G}(\epsilon\xi)=e^{-\pi|\epsilon\xi|^2}$ and $\mathscr{F}^{-1}[G_{\epsilon}](x)=G(\epsilon x)=e^{-\pi|\epsilon x|^2}$
**Proposition.** Let $f\in L^1(\mathbb{R}^n)$ such that $\hat{f}\in L^1(\mathbb{R}^n)$, then$$
\int_{\mathbb{R}^n}\hat{f}(\xi)e^{2\pi ix\cdot\xi}\,d\xi=f(x)\quad a.e.
$$**Proof.** Denote LHS as $\tilde{f}(x)$, and consider$$
\begin{align}
\tilde{f}*G_{\epsilon}(x)&=\int_{\mathbb{R}^n}\hat{f}(\xi)\,d\xi\int_{\mathbb{R}^n}e^{-\pi|\frac{x-y}{\epsilon}|^2}e^{2\pi iy\cdot\xi}\,\frac{dy}{\epsilon^n}\\
&=\int_{\mathbb{R}^n}\hat{f}(\xi)e^{2\pi ix\cdot\xi}\,d\xi\int_{\mathbb{R}^n}e^{-|\frac{u}{\epsilon}|^2}e^{2\pi iu\cdot\xi}\,\frac{du}{\epsilon^n}\\
&=\int_{\mathbb{R}^n}\hat{f}(\xi)e^{2\pi ix\cdot\xi}\mathscr{F}^{-1}[G_{\epsilon}](\xi)\,d\xi\\
&=\int_{\mathbb{R}^n}\hat{f}(\xi)e^{-\pi|\epsilon\xi|^2+2\pi ix\cdot\xi}\,d\xi\\
\end{align}
$$On the other hand, we have$$
\begin{align}
f*G_{\epsilon}(x)&=\int_{\mathbb{R}^n}f(y)G_{\epsilon}(x-y)\,dy\\
&=\int_{\mathbb{R}^n}f(y)\int_{\mathbb{R}^n}\widehat{G_{\epsilon}}(\xi)e^{2\pi i(x-y)\cdot\xi}\,d\xi\,dy\\
&=\int_{\mathbb{R}^n}\widehat{G_{\epsilon}}(\xi)e^{2\pi x\cdot\xi}\,d\xi\int_{\mathbb{R}^n}f(y)e^{-2\pi iy\cdot\xi}\,dy\\
&=\int_{\mathbb{R}^n}\hat{f}(\xi)e^{-\pi|\epsilon\xi|^2+2\pi ix\cdot\xi}\,d\xi
\end{align}
$$Hence we get$$
\tilde{f}*G_{\epsilon}=f*G_{\epsilon}
$$Check that $G$ satisfies the condition in part 1 and recall that $\tilde{f}$ is uniformly continuous and bounded, hence$$
\tilde{f}*G_{\epsilon}\rightrightarrows\tilde{f}\text{ on }\mathbb{R}^n\text{ as }\epsilon\to0^+
$$On the other hand, since $f\in L^1$, $$
f*G_{\epsilon}\to f\; a.e.\text{ on }\mathbb{R}^n
$$Now we conclude that $\tilde{f}=f\;a.e.$ on $\mathbb{R}^n$.$$\tag*{$\square$}$$**Remark.** When I finished the proof I found that this can be simplified. It suffices to calculate $f*G_{\epsilon}$ and apply DCT for RHS. Taking $\epsilon\to0^+$ for the common middle term$$
\int_{\mathbb{R}^n}\hat{f}(\xi)e^{-\pi|\epsilon\xi|^2+2\pi ix\cdot\xi}\,d\xi
$$reveals clearly how the Gaussian kernel restrains both $f$ and $\hat{f}$ on a small region. On the other hand, the interesting fact$$
(\mathscr{F}^{-1}\circ\mathscr{F}[f])*G_{\epsilon}=\tilde{f}*G_{\epsilon}=f*G_{\epsilon}
$$reflects how involution with the Gaussian kernel interacts with Fourier transformation.
**Corollary.** The Fourier transformation is a continuous linear isomorphism from $\mathcal{S}$ (the space of Schwartz functions) to itself.
Here's an alternative proof using generalized functions (also known as distributions). The idea is that to show $\tilde{f}=f$, it suffices to show that $T_{\tilde{f}}=T_f$. The proof is based on the corollary, which can be prove by direct calculations at $x=0$ applying DCT, and then apply $\tau_x$ and known properties of Fourier transformation.
**Alternative Proof.** First we generalize Fourier transformation and the inverse Fourier transformation onto $\mathcal{S}'$. Then for any $T\in \mathcal{S}'$ and $\phi\in\mathcal{S}$,$$
<\mathscr{F}^{-1}[\hat{T}],\phi>=<T,\mathscr{F}^{-1}[\hat{\phi}]>=<T,\phi>
$$so $\mathscr{F}^{-1}[\hat{T}]=T$ by definition of $T$. Apply this to $T_f$, we see that $T_{\tilde{f}}=T_f$. Hence $\tilde{f}=f\;a.e.$.$$\tag*{$\square$}$$**Remark.** This proof is much more elegant, demonstrating the power of generalized functions. Essentially, by the language of generalized functions, we reduce to $f\in\mathcal{S}$.

### E. Poisson's Equity
**Theorem.** Suppose $f$ and $\hat{f}$ are continuous functions on $\mathbb{R}$ controlled by $C(1+|x|^{-(1+\delta)})$, then$$\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}f(x+k)=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}\hat{f}(k)e^{2\pi ikx}
$$Prove involves three steps: prove for $G_{\epsilon}$, for $f_{\epsilon}$, then for $f$. The first step is simply proving the convergence of the Fourier series of $\sum_k e^{-\pi(\frac{x+k}{\epsilon})^2}$. The second step uses DCT to swap integral and sum, so we see that it concludes to the formula for $G_{\epsilon}$. (A perfect example showing how complicated operations are transferred onto the kernel.) The third step uses estimations [here](Controlling%20Convolutions.md) and the M-test to swap $\epsilon\to0^+$ and $\sum_k$.
**Remark.** This formula substituting the Gaussian kernel gives an important symmetry for an important modular function, the Jacobi's $\theta$ function.
