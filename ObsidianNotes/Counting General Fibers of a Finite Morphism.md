#Algebraic-Geometry #Commutative-Algebra 
**Lemma.** Let $B\subset A$ be integral domains with fractional fields $k(B)$ and $k(A)$. If $k(A)/k(B)$ is algebraic (for example, when $A$ is integral over $B$), then elements of $k(A)$ can be written as $a/b$ with $a\in A$ and $b\in B$.
**Proof.** Say $u/v\in k(A)$. Since $k(B)/k(A)$ is algebraic, we can choose$$b_nv^n+b_{n-1}v^{n-1}+\cdots+b_0=0$$with $b_0\neq0$, $b_i\in k(B)$ and hence$$\frac{1}{v}=-\frac{b_nv^{n-1}+b_{n-1}v^{n-2}+\cdots b_1}{b_0}.$$Consequently, by further multiplying by a common denominator of $b_i$'s, $u/v$ has the desired form.$$\tag*{$\square$}$$**Theorem.** $f:X\rightarrow Y$ is a proper surjective morphism between integral schemes of finite type over a field $k$ and $\mathrm{dim}\,X=\mathrm{dim}\,Y$. If $k$ is algebraically closed and $k(A)/k(B)$ is separable (for example, when $\mathrm{char}\,k=0$), then the fiber of a general closed point in $Y$ (i.e. $\exists U\subset Y\,\forall\text{ closed point } y\in U$) is finite and consists of exactly $[k(X):k(Y)]$ points.
**Proof.** Clearly the $f$ is generically finite, so by Hartshorne Ex.II.3.7, there exists an open subset $U\subset Y$ such that the restriction $f^{-1}(U)\rightarrow U$ is finite. Furthermore, by taking open subset of $Y$, we can assume that $X$ and $Y$ are affine. So we have reduced to the purely algebraic settings: $X=\mathrm{Spec}\,A$, $Y=\mathrm{Spec}\,B$, $A$ and $B$ are integral domains of finite type over $k$, $f$ corresponds to an integral inclusion $B\subset A$.
*Let $d:=[k(A):k(B)]$, then since $k(B)/k(A)$ is separable and $k$ is algebraically closed (hence must be infinite), there exists $u\in k(A)$ such that $k(A)=k(B)(u)$.*
By the lemma, we can write $u=a/b$ with $a\in A$ and $b\in B$. By localizing at $b$, we can assume that $u\in A$. Let $f\in k(B)[x]$ be the minimal polynomial of $u$ over $k(B)$, then $\mathrm{deg}\,f=d$.
*Since $k(A)/k(B)$ is separable, $f$ has $d$ distinct roots in its splitting field $F$, say $u_1=u,u_2,\dots,u_d$.*
By further localizing at a common denominator of the coefficients of $f$, we may assume that $f\in B[x]$. Then we can consider the ring homomorphism$$\begin{align}
\varphi:B[x]/(f)&\longrightarrow A\\
\bar{g}&\longmapsto g(u)
\end{align}
$$Clearly this is well-defined. For any $g\in B[x]$, since **f is monic**, there exists $p,q\in B[x]$ such that $g=pf+q$ with $\mathrm{deg}\,q<\mathrm{deg}\,f$. Then $\bar{g}\in\ker\varphi$ implies that $q(u)=0$ and hence $q$ must be 0 by the choice of $f$. So $g\in fB[x]$ and $\varphi$ is injective. The image of $\varphi$ is $B[u]\subset A$. By localizing at the generic point of $B$ and using the lemma, we get an isomorphism $\varphi_{0B}:k(B)[x]/(f)\rightarrow (B-0)^{-1}A=k(A)$. So by localizing at some element of $B$, we can assume that $\varphi$ is an isomorphism. Then $A=B[u]\cong B[x]/(f)$. Now for any maximal ideal $\mathfrak{n}$ of $B$, $A/\mathfrak{n}A\cong (B/\mathfrak{n})[x]/(\bar{f})$.
*Since $f$ has no multiple root, its discriminant $D(f)\neq0\in k(B)$. Since $f\in B[x]$, $D(f)$ actually lies in $B$ (see remark in chapter 4 of [[Summary on Field Extensions]]). If in the first place we localized at the discriminant of $f$, then we have $D(\bar{f})=\overline{D(f)}\neq0\in B/\mathfrak{n}$. So $\bar{f}$ has no multiple root. Since $k$ is algebraically closed, $B/\mathfrak{n}\cong k$ and hence $\bar{f}$ must splits.* Consequently,$$A/\mathfrak{n}A\cong k[x]/\prod_{i=1}^d(x-\bar{u}_i)\cong\prod_{i=1}^dk.$$The fiber of $\mathfrak{n}$ is the spectrum of $A/\mathfrak{n}A$, which is an Artin ring since $B\subset A$ is integral. Let $\mathfrak{m}_1,\dots,\mathfrak{m}_r$ be the prime ideals of $A$ over $\mathfrak{n}$, then we need to show that $r=d$. By the structure theorem for artinian rings, if $\bigcap_{i=1}^r\mathfrak{m}_i^n\subset\mathfrak{n}A$, then $A/\mathfrak{n}A\cong \prod_{i=1}^rA/(\mathfrak{n}A+\mathfrak{m}_i^n).$ If we can show that $A/\mathfrak{n}A$ is reduced, i.e. $\bigcap_i\mathfrak{m}_i=\mathfrak{n}A$, then$$A/\mathfrak{n}A\cong\prod_{i=1}^rA/\mathfrak{m}_i.$$*Since $k$ is algebraically closed,  this implies that $\mathrm{dim}_k\,A/\mathfrak{n}A=r$. But we have seen above that $A\cong\prod_{i=1}^dk$ is reduced indeed, so $r=\mathrm{dim}_k\,A/\mathfrak{n}A=d$.*$$\tag*{$\square$}$$**Remark.**  We have marked places using hypothesis on $k$ and $k(A)/k(B)$. Now we try to generalize.
1. We first assume that $k(A)/k(B)$ is not separable. Then $\mathrm{charI}\,k(B)=p>0$. If $k(A)/k(B)$ were not separable, primitive element theorem wouldn't be available. However, by induction on $d$, we can reduce to the simple extension case.
2. By 6.3 of [[Summary on Field Extensions]], we can write$$f=\prod_{i=1}^s(x-u_i)^{p^m}=g(x^{p^m}),$$where $s=[k(B)(u):k(B)]_s$ and $p^m=[k(B)(u):k(B)]_i$. So we should replace $D(f)$ with $D(g)$. And the isomorphism $$A/\mathfrak{n}A\cong (B/\mathfrak{n})[t]/(\bar{f})$$still holds. If $k$ is algebraically closed, then $A/\mathfrak{n}A\cong (B/\mathfrak{n})[t]/\prod_i^s(t-\bar{u}_i)^{p^m}$. So in the simple extension case, $\#f^{-1}(y)=[k(A):k(B)]_s$ for a general closed point $y\in Y$ and each $x\in f^{-1}(y)$ has a nonreduced local ring isomorphic to $k[t]/(t^{p^m})$.
3. For general extensions, we need to consider an extension tower$$B=C_0\subset C_1\subset\cdots\subset C_n\subset A$$with $C_i=C_{i-1}[u_i]$ and then localize at some $g\in B$ so that $A_g=(C_n)_g=B_g[u_1,\dots,u_n]$. Notice also that the $\#f^{-1}(y)$ and $[k(A):k(B)]_s$ are both multiplicative.
4. So we have proved that if $k$ is closed and $k(A)/k(B)$ arbitrary, then $\#f^{-1}(y)=[k(A):k(B)]_s$.
5. In order to have a better understanding of the reduced structure, we prove in a more elegant way: first introduce the separable closure $S$, which is necessarily simple over $k(B)$, then $k(A)/S$ is purely inseparable. The intermediate ring would be the integral closure $C$ of $B$ in $S$. The purely inseparable part is dealt with a tower of purely inseparable simple extensions.

>[!tip] Useful Tricks
>1. It's always a good idea to prove for the characteristic zero and algebraically closed case first. 
>2. We have a great understanding of artinian rings and modules! Many numeric invariants can be derived or connected using them.
>3. Notice how we obtained a ring isomorphism out of a field isomorphism. Certain tricks are useful, such as Euclidean algorithm for monic polynomials, things (especially those expressible as vanishing of kernel, cokernel and image) happening on the generic point are also happening on an open subset (as long as certain finite conditions are satisfied).
>4. Notice how we used discriminant!
