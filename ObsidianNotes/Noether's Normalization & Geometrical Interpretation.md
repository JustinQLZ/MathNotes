## Noether's Normalization
**Theorem (Noether's Normalization Lemma)** Let $k$ be a field, and let $A\neq 0$ be a finitely generated $k$-algebra, then there exists elements $y_1, ..., y_r \in A$ which are algebraically independent over $k$ and such that $A$ is integral over $k[y_1, ..., y_r]$.
**Proof when $k$ is infinite.** Let $x_1, ..., x_n$ generate $A$ as  a $k$-algebra. We can renumber them so that $x_1, ..., x_r$ are algebraically independent over $k$ and each of $x_{r+1}, ..., x_n$ is algebraic over $k[x_1, ..., x_n]$. Now proceed by induction on $n$. If $n = r$ there's nothing to do, so suppose $n > r$ and the result true for $n-1$ generators. The generator $x_n$ is algebraic over $k[x_1, ..., x_{n-1}]$, hence there exists a polynomial $f$ in $n$ variables such that $f(x_1, ..., x_{n-1}, x_n)=0$. Let $F$ be the homogeneous part of highest degree in $f$. Since $k$ is infinite, there exists $\lambda_1, ..., \lambda_{n-1}\in k$ such that $F(\lambda_1, ..., \lambda_{n-1}, 1) \neq 0$. Put $x_i'=x_i-\lambda_ix_n \,(1\leq i\leq n-1)$. Notice that the coefficient of the term with highest degree in $x_n$ in $f(x_1', ..., x_{n-1}', x_n)$ is precisely $F(\lambda_1, ..., \lambda_{n-1}, 1)$, so dividing by this non-zero element we have proved that $x_n$ is integral over $A'=k[x_1', ..., x_{n-1}']$. Then the proofs follows from applying the inductive hypothesis to $A'$. $$\tag*{$\square$}$$
This could be used to prove **the weak form of Hilbert's Nullensatz Theorem**, see [[Proofs of Hilbert's Nullensatz]].
## Geometrical Interpretation
From the proof it follows that $y_1, ..., y_r$ may be chosen to be linear combinations of $x_1, ..., x_n$. This has the following geometrical interpretation: if $k$ is algebraically closed and $X$ is an affine algebraic variety in $k^n$ with coordinate ring $A\neq0$, then there exists a linear subspace $L$ of dimension $r$ and a linear mapping of $k^n$ onto $L$ which maps $X$ onto $L$. In particular, this gives a geometrical intuition that algebraic varieties over an algebraically closed field, over $\mathbb{C}$ for example,  can't be "bounded". To show this, first we need a lemma:
**Lemma** Let $A$ be a subring of a ring $B$ such that $B$ is integral over $A$, and let $f:A\rightarrow\Omega$ be a homomorphism of rings, where $\Omega$ is an algebraically closed field. Then $f$ can be extended to $B$.
**Sketch of proof.** Partially order $\Sigma = \{(A', f') : A \subset A' \subset B \,\text{and}\, f' \,\text{extends}\, f\}$ by $(A_1, f_1) \leq (A_2, f_2) \iff A_1\subset A_2 \,\text{and}\, f_2|_{A_1}=f_1$, then use **Zorn's Lemma** to show that there's a maximal element $(A', f')\in\Sigma$. Suppose $A'\subsetneq B$, then $\exists x\in B-A'$. Let $A''=A'[x]$, and denote $\mathfrak{p}=ker\,f'$. $\mathfrak{p}\in SpecA'$ since $\Omega$ is a field. $B$ is integral over $A$ implies that $A''$ is integral over $A'$, so $\exists\mathfrak{q}\in SpecA'' \,s.t.\,\mathfrak{q}\cap A'=\mathfrak{p}$. Consider the commutative diagram
$$
\begin{CD}
A' @>\subset>>                   A''\\
@VVV                             @VVV\\
A'/\mathfrak{p} @>\subset>>      A''/\mathfrak{q}\\
@VVV                             @VVV\\
\kappa(\mathfrak{p}) @>\subset>> \kappa(\mathfrak{q})\\
\end{CD}
$$
and identify $A'/\mathfrak{p}$ with $im\,f\subset \Omega$, then $\kappa(\mathfrak{p})$ maybe considered as a subfield of $\Omega$. Show that $A''/\mathfrak{q}=(A'/\mathfrak{p})[\bar{x}]$, so $\kappa(\mathfrak{q})=\kappa(\mathfrak{p})(\bar{x})$. $x$ is integral over $A'$ implies that its image in $\kappa(\mathfrak{q})$ is algebraic over $\kappa(\mathfrak{p})$, so $\kappa(\mathfrak{q})/\kappa(\mathfrak{p})$ is an algebraic extension. Since $\Omega$ is algebraically closed, we may assume that $\kappa(\mathfrak{q})$ is a subfield of $\Omega$. Then the composition $A''\rightarrow A''/\mathfrak{q}\rightarrow\kappa(\mathfrak{q})\rightarrow\Omega$ extends $f'$, which contradicts the choice of $(A', f')$. So we must have $A'=B$, which completes the proof.$$\tag*{$\square$}$$
**Proof of the geometrical interpretation.** Assume $y_i=T_i(x_1, ..., x_n)\,(1\leq i\leq r)$ and let $T=(T_1, ..., T_r):k^n\rightarrow L=k^r$, then $T$ is a linear mapping. The $y_i$'s are algebraically independent over $k$, so they're linearly independent over $k$ and $T$ is surjective. $\forall l\in L$, let $v_l:k[y_1, ..., y_r]\rightarrow k$ be the valuation at $l$. Then by the **Lemma**, $v_l$ extends to $A$. Now consider the composition $v':k[x_1, ..., x_n]\longrightarrow A\stackrel{v_l}{\longrightarrow}k\,$, it is surjective, so $ker\,v'$ is a maximal ideal of $k[x_1, ..., x_n]$ and defines a point $a=(v'(x_1), ..., v'(x_n))\in k^n$ such that $ker\,v'=(x_1-a_1, ..., x_n-a_n)$. Since $A=k[x_1, ..., x_n]/I(X)\,$, $\forall f\in I(X)\enspace f(a)=0$ and hence $a\in X$. $T_i(a)=T_i(v'(x_1), ..., v'(x_n))=l_i\,$, so $T(a)=l$ and $T|_X$ is surjective.$$\tag*{$\square$}$$
Further more, $\forall l\in L\enspace\#T^{-1}(l) \cap X$ is finite and bounded.
**Proof.** Suppose $A=k[y_1, ..., y_n]$ where $y_1, ..., y_r$ are algebraically independent over $k$ and $y_{r+1}, ..., y_{n}$ are integral over $k[y_1, ..., y_r]$, then there exists monic polynomials $f_1, ..., f_{n-r}\in k[y_1, ..., y_r][Y]$ such that $f_i(y_1, ..., y_r, y_{r+i})=0\,(1\leq i\leq n-r)$. We may assume that $x_1, ..., x_n\in k[x_1, ..., x_n]$ are a set of coordinate functions of $k^n$ whose images in $A$ are $y_1, ..., y_n$ respectively, then $\forall l\in L\,\forall a\in T^{-1}\cap X\enspace f_i(a_1, ..., a_r)=0\enspace (1\leq i\leq n-r)$, since $a\in X\implies v_a \text{ factors through } A$. So $\#T^{-1}(l) \cap X$ is bounded by $\Pi_{i=1}^{n-r}deg_Y\,f_i$.$$\tag*{$\square$}$$
## Proof for General $k$
The failure of the previous proof for possibly finite $k$ is due to the possible non-existence of the $\lambda$'s. However, for the proof of Noether's Normalization, we only need to find a change of variables such that after this change the $f$ in previous proof can be made monic in $x_n$. This is totally possible so the failure is not fatal if we accept non-linear change of variables.
**Proof.** Let $N\in\mathbb{N}$ be larger than the total degree of $g$ and consider the following change of variables: $x_i\mapsto x_i+x_n^{N^m} \text{ for }i<n \text{ and }x_n\mapsto x_n$. Next we show that $f$ becomes monic in $x_n$. If we look at each non-zero term in $f$, after this change of variable, it will expand to give a unique largest degree term which is a nonzero constant times $x_n$ raised to some power; moreover, these power-of-$x_n$ terms will be all distinct. Therefore there is a largest one and $f$ can be made monic in $x_n$ by dividing its coefficient.$$\tag*{$\square$}$$
See [[Normalization As A Dimension Trick]] for its applications in dimension theories, and **Theorem 3.** for a generalization.
