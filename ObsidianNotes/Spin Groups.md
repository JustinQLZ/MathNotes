#Algebra #Lie-Group-and-Lie-Algebra 
### Basics
#### Real Clifford Algebra
Given a $\mathbb{R}$-vector space $V$ with a metric $\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle$. The Clifford algebra $Cl(V)$ generated by $V$ is the quotient of $T(V)$ (the tensor algebra) by the two-sided ideal generated by all elements of the form $$v\otimes v+||v||^2\cdot 1.$$Note that since $u\wedge v=\frac{1}{2}(u\otimes v-v\otimes u)$, we have$$
uv=-\langle u,v\rangle+u\wedge v.
$$Choosing a basis of $V$, we can show that $\mathrm{dim}_{\mathbb{R}}\,Cl(V)=2^{d}$ where $d=\mathrm{dim}_{\mathbb{R}}\,V$.

**Remark.** Fixing an oriented orthonormal basis is very useful for computational purposes. Also, we can view $Cl(V)$ as being given by a new multiplication on $\bigwedge^*(V)$. This new multiplication is generated by$$
v\cdot(v_1\wedge\cdots\wedge v_k)=v\wedge v_1\wedge\cdots\wedge v_k-v\lrcorner(v_1\wedge\cdots\wedge v_k),
$$where$$
v\lrcorner(v_1\wedge\cdots v_k)=\sum_{i=1}^k(-1)^{i-1}\langle v,v_i\rangle v_1\wedge\cdots\wedge \hat{v}_i\wedge\cdots\wedge v_k.
$$
Note that the grading on $T(V)$ descends to a $\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$-grading on $Cl(V)$, giving a decomposition$$
Cl(V)=Cl_0(V)\oplus Cl_1(V).$$Denote by$$
\epsilon:Cl(V)\rightarrow Cl(V)$$the algebra homomorphism that is multiplication by $+1$ on $Cl_0(V)$ and is multiplication by $-1$ on $Cl_1(V)$; i.e. $\epsilon(v_0+v_1)=v_0-v_1$.

Notice that $Cl_0(V)$ is isomorphic to $Cl(W)$ for a codimension 1 subspace $W\subset V$.
#### Spin Groups
Let $Cl^{\times}(V)$ denote the multiplicative group of units of $Cl(V)$. We define the group $Pin(V)$ as the subgroup of $Cl^{\times}(V)$ generated by elements $v\in V$ with $||v||^2=1$ (which are units indeed). Let $Spin(V)=Pin(V)\cap Cl_0(V$). By this definition, $Cl(V)$ (resp. $Cl_0(V)$) is the smallest $\mathbb{R}$-algebra containing $Pin(V)$ (resp. $Spin(V)$) as a subgroup of its multiplicative group of units. Notice that since $\mathrm{det}\neq0$ is an open condition, $\mathrm{dim}\,Cl^{\times}(V)=2^d$. But as we will see, $\mathrm{dim}\,Pin(V)=\mathrm{dim}\,O(V)=\frac{d(d-1)}{2}$.

The natural action of $O(n)$ on $V$ extends to an action on $Cl(V)$. This action is effective and hence induces an embedding of $O(V)$ into the algebra automorphisms of $Cl(V)$. The group $Pin(V)$ acts on $Cl(V)$ via conjugation$$
\sigma\cdot c=\sigma c\sigma^{-1}.
$$This action preserves the algebra structure and its restriction on $Spin(V)$ further preserves the $\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$-grading. Note that for any $w\in V$ with $||w||=1$ and $v\in V$, we have$$
wvw^{-1}=-R_{w^{\perp}}(v),
$$where $R_{w^{\perp}}$ is the reflection along the hyperplane perpendicular to $w$. It follows that the image of this action lies in $O(V)$ and induces $Spin(V)\rightarrow SO(V)$. Clearly this map is surjective and its kernel is $Spin(V)\cap Z(Cl(V))$. Computing with basis, we have$$
Z(Cl(V))=\begin{cases}
\mathbb{R}\subset Cl_0(V),&d\text{ is even};\\
\mathbb{R}\oplus\mathbb{R}e_1\cdots e_d,&d\text{ is odd}.
\end{cases}
$$So in both cases, its kernel is $\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}=\{\pm1\}$. (Not true for $Pin(V)\rightarrow O(V)$ though.) By reducing to $d=2$, we can show that if $d\geq2$, then $Spin(V)\rightarrow SO(V)$ is a nontrivial double covering, and hence $Spin(V)$ is compact. ($Pin(V)$ is compact, too.) So $\mathrm{dim}\,Spin(V)=\frac{d(d-1)}{2}$. Furthermore, since $\pi_1(SO(V))=\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$ for $d\geq 3$, in this case, $Spin(V)\rightarrow SO(V)$ is the universal covering.

### Representations of Spin Groups
#### Complexification of the Clifford Algebra
Let $Cl(V)_{\mathbb{C}}=Cl(V)\otimes_{\mathbb{R}}\mathbb{C}$ be the complexification of $Cl(V)$. Fix an oriented orthonormal basis $\{e_1,\dots,e_d\}$, define$$
\omega_{\mathbb{C}}=i^{[\frac{d+1}{2}]}e_1\cdots e_d.
$$Verify that $\omega_{\mathbb{C}}$ is independent of the choice of basis and $\omega_{\mathbb{C}}^2=1$. Thus we can split $Cl(V)_{\mathbb{C}}$ into eigenspaces of the action of $\omega_{\mathbb{C}}$ by left multiplication:$$
Cl(V)_{\mathbb{C}}=Cl(V)_{\mathbb{C}}^+\oplus Cl(V)_{\mathbb{C}}^-.
$$
If $d$ is odd, then $\omega_{\mathbb{C}}$ is in the center of $Cl(V)_\mathbb{C}$, and interchanges $Cl_0(V)_\mathbb{C}$ and $Cl_0(V)_\mathbb{C}$. This means the decomposition above is an orthogonal decomposition into subalgebras. So in this case, $Cl_0(V)_\mathbb{C}$ intersects $Cl(V)_\mathbb{C}^\pm$ trivially, and$$
Cl_0(V)_{\mathbb{C}}\hookrightarrow Cl(V)_\mathbb{C}\xrightarrow{\pi^\pm}Cl(V)_\mathbb{C}^\pm
$$are isomorphisms.

If $d$ is even, then $\omega_{\mathbb{C}}$ is in the center of $Cl_0(V)_{\mathbb{C}}$ but anti-commutes with elements of $Cl_1(V)_\mathbb{C}$. Hence it induces a splitting $Cl_0(V)_\mathbb{C}=Cl_0(V)_\mathbb{C}^+\oplus Cl_0(V)_\mathbb{C}^-$. Under the isomorphism of $Cl_0(V)$ with $Cl(W)$ for a codimension 1 subspace $W\subset V$, this decomposition agrees with the decomposition given before in the odd dimensional case. In particular, $Cl_0(V)^\pm_\mathbb{C}$ are isomorphic. Consequently, $Cl_0(V)_\mathbb{C}^+$ is isomorphic to $Cl(W)_\mathbb{C}$ for a codimension 2 subspace $W\subset V$.
Let $v_1,\dots,v_d$ be an orthonormal basis for $V$, and $e_1,e_2$ an orthonormal basis for $\mathbb{R}^2$. The real linear map$$
V\oplus\mathbb{R}^2\rightarrow Cl(V)_\mathbb{C}\otimes Cl(\mathbb{R^2})_\mathbb{C}
$$given by $v_j\mapsto iv_j\otimes e_1e_2$ and $e_i\mapsto 1\otimes e_i$ extends to an algebra isomorphism$$
Cl(V\oplus\mathbb{R}^2)_\mathbb{C}\xrightarrow{\cong}Cl(V)_\mathbb{C}\otimes Cl(\mathbb{R^2})_\mathbb{C}.
$$Also, 
1. $Cl(\mathbb{R})\cong\mathbb{C}$ and hence $Cl(\mathbb{R})_\mathbb{C}\cong\mathbb{C}\oplus\mathbb{C}$.
2. $Cl(\mathbb{R}^2)\cong\mathbb{H}$ and hence $Cl(\mathbb{R}^2)_\mathbb{C}\cong\mathbb{H}\otimes\mathbb{C}$. Denote $\mathbb{C}[2]$ the algebra of $2\times 2$ matrices. We define a $\mathbb{R}$-linear map $\mathbb{H}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}[2]$ given by sending $\alpha+j\beta$ to$$
\begin{pmatrix}
\alpha & -\bar{\beta}\\
\beta & \bar{\alpha}
\end{pmatrix}.
$$Writing $x+jy\in\mathbb{H}$ for $x,y\in\mathbb{C}$ as a column vector, this matrix gives the action of $\alpha+j\beta$ by left multiplication on $\mathbb{H}$. Extending scalars we get an isomorphism of $\mathbb{C}$-algebras$$
\mathbb{H}\otimes\mathbb{C}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}[2].
$$
From these, we can construct complexifications of all Clifford algebras. If $d=2n$, then $Cl(V)\otimes_\mathbb{C}$ is isomorphic to the matrix algebra $\mathbb{C}[2^n]$. If $d=2n+1$, then $Cl(V)_\mathbb{C}$ is isomorphic as an algebra to the direct sum of two copies of $\mathbb{C}[2^n]$. Consequently, if $d=2n$, then $Cl_0(V)_\mathbb{C}^+$ is isomorphic to the matrix algebra $\mathbb{C}[2^{n-1}]$.
#### Complex Spin Representations
An immediate consequence of the previous section is the classification of complex irreducible representations of $Cl(V)$.
If $d=2n$, then $Cl(V)$ has a unique irreducible finite dimensional complex representation $S_\mathbb{C}(V)$ up to isomorphism. Any such representation has dimension $2^n$. The action of $Cl(V)_\mathbb{C}$ on $S_\mathbb{C}(V)$ induces an isomorphism$$
Cl(V)_\mathbb{C}\rightarrow\mathrm{End}_\mathbb{C}(S_\mathbb{C}(V))=S_\mathbb{C}(V)\otimes_\mathbb{C}S_\mathbb{C}(V)^*.
$$If $d=2n+1$, then $Cl(V)$ has exactly two irreducible finite dimensional complex representations up to isomorphism. These induces isomorphic representations $S_\mathbb{C}(V)$ of $Cl_0(V)$ by restriction. Any such representation has dimension $2^n$, and the action of Clifford multiplication induces an isomorphism$$
Cl_0(V)_\mathbb{C}\rightarrow\mathrm{End}_\mathbb{C}(S_\mathbb{C}(V))=S_\mathbb{C}(V)\otimes_\mathbb{C}S_\mathbb{C}(V)^*.
$$
When $d=2n$, $S_\mathbb{C}$ decomposes into two $Cl_0(V)_\mathbb{C}$-modules $S_\mathbb{C}^{\pm}$ according to the action of $\omega_\mathbb{C}$. The action of $Cl_1(V)_\mathbb{C}$ interchanges them. And we have the following isomorphisms:$$
\begin{align}
Cl_0(V)_\mathbb{C}^+&\cong\mathrm{End}_\mathbb{C}(S^+_\mathbb{C}(V))\\
Cl_0(V)_\mathbb{C}^-&\cong\mathrm{End}_\mathbb{C}(S^-_\mathbb{C}(V))\\
Cl_1(V)_\mathbb{C}^-&\cong\mathrm{Hom}_\mathbb{C}(S^+_\mathbb{C}(V),S^-_\mathbb{C}(V))\\
Cl_1(V)_\mathbb{C}^+&\cong\mathrm{Hom}_\mathbb{C}(S^-_\mathbb{C}(V),S^+_\mathbb{C}(V))
\end{align}
$$Consequently, $S_\mathbb{C}^\pm$ are the two irreducible complex representations of $Cl_0(V)_\mathbb{C}$ up to isomorphism.

There is a unique complex representation of $Spin(V)$ (up to isomorphism) induced from any irreducible complex finite dimensional representation of $Cl(V)$. This representation is called the complex spin representation and is denoted$$
\Delta_\mathbb{C}:Spin(V)\rightarrow\mathrm{Aut}_\mathbb{C}(S_\mathbb{C}(V)).
$$
If $d=2n$, then this representation decomposes into two inequivalent irreducible representations $S_\mathbb{C}^+(V)$ and $S_\mathbb{C}^-(V)$ of $Spin(V)$, each of dimension $2^{n-1}$. These representations of $Spin(V)$ are denoted by $\Delta_\mathbb{C}^\pm$. If the $d=2n+1$, then $\Delta_\mathbb{C}$ is an irreducible representation of $Spin(V)$ of dimension $2^n$.

### Example of $d=3$.
$Cl_3\cong \mathbb{H}\oplus\mathbb{H}$ as $\mathbb{R}$-algebras. $Cl_0(\mathbb{R}^3)$ is the diagonal copy of $\mathbb{H}$. The group $Spin(3)$ is the group of units of norm one in $Cl_0(\mathbb{R}^3)=\mathbb{H}$. (Verify by dimensional argument.) We have $Spin(3)\cong SU(2)\cong S^3$. The action of $Spin(3)\cong SU(2)$ on $\mathbb{R}^3$ is the usual adjoint action of $SU(2)$ on its Lie algebra $\mathfrak{su}(2)\cong\mathfrak{so}(3)\cong\mathbb{R}^3$ and the image of this representation is $SO(3)$. The spin representation $\Delta_\mathbb{C}$ is the standard representation of $SU(2)$ on $\mathbb{C}^2$.

Recall the Pauli matrices$$
\sigma_1=\begin{pmatrix}
0 & 1\\
1 & 0
\end{pmatrix},\quad
\sigma_2=\begin{pmatrix}
0 & -i\\
i & 0
\end{pmatrix},\quad
\sigma_3=\begin{pmatrix}
1 & 0\\
0 & -1
\end{pmatrix}.
$$Let $T_j=\frac{i\sigma_j}{2}$. Then $T_1,T_2,T_3$ are a orthonormal basis under the Killing form $\langle X,Y\rangle=-2\mathrm{tr}(XY)$. The adjoint representation of $SU(2)$ on $\mathfrak{su}(2)$ is given by $Ad_U(X)=UXU^H$.
