## Hilbert's Nullensatz
**Theorem 1. (Zariski's Lemma)** Let $k$ be a field, $E$ a finitely generated $k$-algebra. If $E$ is a field, then it is a finite algebraic extension of $k$. In particular, if $k$ is algebraically closed, then  $E\cong k$.

**Corollary.** Let $k$ be an algebraically closed field, and $\mathfrak{m}\subset k[x_1, \dots, x_n]$ a maximal ideal of $A$, then $k[x_1, \dots, x_n]/\mathfrak{m}\cong k$ and $\exists a_1, \dots, a_n\in k\enspace\mathfrak{m}=(x_1-a_1, \dots, x_n-a_n)$.
**Proof** Denote $A=k[x_1, \dots, x_n]$, then by **Theorem 1**, $A/\mathfrak{m}\cong k$. Denote $v:A\rightarrow A/\mathfrak{m}\cong k$, and let $a_i=v(x_i)\in k\,(1\leq i\leq n)$. Then clearly $(x_1-a_1, \dots, x_n-a_n)\subset ker\,v=\mathfrak{m}$. Conversely, $\forall f\in \mathfrak{m}$ divide $f$ by $x_1-a_1$. $v(f)=f(a_1, \dots, a_n)=0\implies\exists f_1\in k[x_2, \dots, x_n]\text{ and }g_1\in k[x_1, \dots, x_n]\enspace s.t.\,f=(x_1-a_1)g_1+f_1$, where $f_1(a_2, \dots, a_n)=0$. Repeat the process we get $g_1, \dots, g_n\in A\enspace s.t.\,f=(x_1-a_1)g_1+\dots+(x_n-a_n)g_n\,$, so $f\in (x_1-a_1, \dots, x_n-a_n)$. $$\tag*{$\square$}$$
**Theorem 2. (weak form)** Let $k$ be an  algebraically closed field, and let $\mathfrak{a}\subset k[x_1, \dots, x_n]$ be an ideal such that $Z(\mathfrak{a})=\varnothing$, then $\mathfrak{a}=(1)$.
**Proof assuming Theorem 1.** If $\mathfrak{a}\neq(1)$, then $A=k[x_1, \dots, x_n]/\mathfrak{a}\neq0$, hence there exists a maximal ideal $\mathfrak{m}\subset A$. By the **Corollary**, $A/\mathfrak{m}\cong k$ and $\exists a_1, \dots, a_n\in k\enspace\mathfrak{m}=(x_1-a_1, \dots, x_n-a_n)$. Verify that $(a_1, \dots, a_n)\in Z(\mathfrak{m}\cap k[x_1, \dots, x_n])\subset Z(\mathfrak{a})$.$$\tag*{$\square$}$$
**Proof using Noether's Normalization.** If $\mathfrak{a}\neq(1)$, then $A=k[x_1, \dots, x_n]/\mathfrak{a}\neq0$, hence by **Noether's Normalization** (see [[Noether's Normalization & Geometrical Interpretation]]), there exists a $k$-linear subspace $L$ in $k^n$ and a mapping of $Z(\mathfrak{a})$ onto $L$. $dim_kL\geq0$, so $Z(\mathfrak{a})\neq\varnothing$.$$\tag*{$\square$}$$

**Theorem 3. (strong form)** Let $k$ be an  algebraically closed field, and let $\mathfrak{a}\subset k[x_1, \dots, x_n]$ be an ideal, then $I(Z(\mathfrak{a}))=r(\mathfrak{a})$ (the nilradical of $\mathfrak{a}$).
**Proof assuming Theorem 2, using the Rabinowitsch trick.** $\forall f\in r(\mathfrak{a})\,\exists n\in\mathbb{N}\enspace f^n\in\mathfrak{a}\implies\forall P\in Z(\mathfrak{a})\enspace f^n(P)=0\implies f(P)=0\implies f\in I(Z(\mathfrak{a}))$, so $r(\mathfrak{a})\subset I(Z(\mathfrak{a}))$.
Conversely, given $f\in I(Z(\mathfrak{a}))$, we want to show that $\exists n\in\mathbb{N}\enspace s.t.\,f^n\in\mathfrak{a}$. ==Our general strategy for doing so is a common one in mathematics: we consider a related object in a higher dimensional space and project back down to the space we actually care about.==
Specifically, we construct a related ideal, $\mathfrak{a}^*$ in a larger polynomial ring by adding another indeterminate, which we call $y$. We do so in a way that guarantees that $V(\mathfrak{a}^*)=\varnothing$, so by **Theorem 2**, $\mathfrak{a}^*=k[x_1, \dots, x_n, y]$. This allows us to construct an expression for $f^n$ in terms of products of generators of $\mathfrak{a}$, which implies that $f^n\in\mathfrak{a}$.
By [[Hilbert's Basis Theorem]], $k[x_1, \dots, x_n]$ is Noetherian, which implies that $\mathfrak{a}$ is finitely generated, say by $f_1, \dots, f_k$. Consider $\mathfrak{a}^*\subset k[x_1, \dots, x_n, y]$ generated by $f_1, \dots, f_k, 1-yf$.  Suppose $\exists P\in Z(\mathfrak{a}^*)$, then $f(P)=0$. But this implies that $P$ can't be a zero of $1-yf$, contradiction. So $Z(\mathfrak{a}^*)=\varnothing$ and by **Theorem 2**, $\mathfrak{a}^*=k[x_1, \dots, x_n, y]$. In particular, $1\in\mathfrak{a}^*$. So $\exists g_1, \dots, g_k,g\in k[x_1, \dots, x_n, y]\enspace s.t.\,1=g_1f_1+,\dots,+g_kf_k+g(1-yf)$. Now consider the homomorphism $\psi:k[x_1, \dots, x_n, y]\rightarrow k(x_1, \dots, x_n)$ with $y\mapsto 1/f$. Then $1=\psi(1)=\psi(g_1)f_1+,\dots,+\psi(g_k)f_k$. Let $n$ be the maximum degree of $y$ that appears in the denominators of $q_i$, then multiplying by $f^n$, we get $f^n=g_1^*f_1+, \dots, +g_k^*f_k\in\mathfrak{a}$, so $f\in r(\mathfrak{a})$.$$\tag*{$\square$}$$
Here's another formalization of the **Rabinowitsch trick**: to show that $f\in r(\mathfrak{a})$ is equivalent to show that $0=(k[x_1, \dots, x_n]/\mathfrak{a})_f\cong k[x_1, \dots, x_n, y]/(\mathfrak{a}, 1-yf)$, the proof then follows naturally from **Theorem 2**. 
Now it remains to prove **Theorem 1**.
## 1st Proof
**Proposition.** Let $A\subset B\subset C$ be rings. Suppose that $A$ is Noetherian, that $C$ is finitely generated as an $A$-algebra and that $C$ is either finitely generated as a $B$-module or, equivalently, integral  over $B$. Then $B$ is finitely generated as an $A$-algebra.
**Proof.** Let $x_1, \dots, x_m$ generate $C$ as an $A$-algebra, and let $y_1, \dots, y_n$ generated $C$ as a $B$-module. Then there exists expressions of the form
$$
\begin{align}
x_i&=\Sigma_jb_{ij}y_j\quad(b_{ij}\in B)\tag{1}\\
y_iy_j&=\Sigma_kb_{ijk}y_k\quad(b_{ijk}\tag{2}\in B)
\end{align}
$$
Let $B_0$ be the algebra generated over $A$ by the $b_{ij}$ and the $b_{ijk}\,$. Since $A$ is Noetherian, so is $B_0$, and $A\subset B_0\subset B$.
Any element of $C$ is a polynomial in the $x_i$ with coefficients in $A$. Substituting $(1)$, and making repeated use of $(2)$ shows that each element of $C$ is a linear combination of the $y_i$ with coefficients in $B_0$, and hence $C$ is finitely generated as a $B_0$-module. Since $B_0$ is Noetherian, and $B$ is a submodule of $C$, if follows that $B$ is finitely generated as a $B_0$-module. Since $B_0$ is finitely generated as an $A$-algebra, it follows that $B$ is finitely generated as an $A$-algebra. $$\tag*{$\square$}$$
**Proof of Theorem 1.** Let $E=k[x_1, \dots, x_n]$. If $E$ is not algebraic over $k$ then we can renumber the $x_i$ so that $x_1, \dots, x_r$ are algebraically independent over $k$, where $r\geq 1$, and each of $x_{r+1}, \dots, x_n$ is algebraic over the field $F=k(x_1, \dots, x_r)$. Hence $E$ is a finite algebraic extension of $F$ and therefore finitely generated as an $F$-module. Applying previous **Proposition** to $k\subset F\subset E$ show that $F$ is a finitely generated $k$-algebra., say $F=k[y_1, \dots, y_s]$. Each $y_j$ is of the form $f_j/g_j$, where $f_j$ and $g_j$ are polynomials in $x_1, \dots, x_r$.
Now there are infinitely many irreducible polynomials in the ring $k[x_1, \dots, x_r]$, for example we could apply Euclid's proof of the existence of infinitely many prime numbers. Hence there is an irreducible polynomial $h$ which is prime to each of the $g_j$ (for example, $h=g_1\dots g_s+1$ would do) and the element $h^{-1}$ of $F$ could not be a polynomial in the $y_j$. This is a contradiction. Hence $E$ is algebraic over $k$, and therefore finite algebraic. $$\tag*{$\square$}$$
## 2nd Proof
This proof is due to Zariski and uses an idea similar to that of the first one.
**Proof of Theorem 1.** Let $x_1, \dots, x_n$ generate $E$ as a $k$-algebra. The proof is by induction on $n$. When $n=1$, $x_1$ must be algebraic over $k$, otherwise $E\cong k[X]$ and $E$ can't be a field. Now assume $n>1$, let $A=k[x_1]\subset E$ and let $F=k(x_1)=FracA\subset E$. By the inductive hypothesis, $E/F$ is a finite algebraic extension, hence each $x_2, \dots, x_n$ satisfies a monic polynomial equation with coefficients in $F$, i.e. coefficients of the form $f/g$ where $f,g\in k[x_1]$. Let $a\in k[x_1]$ be the product of there denominators, then each of $x_2, \dots, x_n$ is integral over $A_f$. Hence $E$ and therefore $F$ is integral over $A_f$.
Suppose $x_1$ is transcendental over $k$, then $A$ is integrally closed since it's a UFD. Hence $A_f$ is integrally closed. (The integral closure of $A_f$ in $Frac\,A_f=Frac\,A=F$ is the localization of the integral closure of $A$ in $Frac\,A$ by $f$, which is $A_f$.) Therefore $A_f=F$, which is clearly absurd. Hence $x_1$ is algebraic over $k$, hence $F$ and therefore $E$ is a finite algebraic extension of $k$.$$\tag*{$\square$}$$
## 3rd Proof
**Proposition.** Let $A\subset B$ be integral domains, $B$ finitely generated over $A$. Let $v\in B-\{0\}$, then there exists $u\in A-\{0\}$ with the following property: any homomorphism $f:A\rightarrow \Omega$, where $\Omega$ is an algebraically closed field such that $f(u)\neq 0$ can be extended to a homomorphism $g:B\rightarrow \Omega$ such that $g(v)\neq0$.
**Proof.** By induction on the number of generators of $B$ over $A$, we reduce to the case where $B=A[x]$.
*Case 1: $x$ is transcendental over $A$.* Let $v=a_0x^n+a_1x^{n-1}+\dots+a_n$, and take $u=a_0$. Then if $f:A\rightarrow \Omega$ is such that $f(u)\neq0$, there exists $\xi\in\Omega$ such that $f(a_0)\xi^n+f(a_1)\xi^{n-1}+\dots+f(a_n)\neq0$, because $\Omega$ is infinite. Define $g:B\rightarrow \Omega$ extending $f$ by putting $g(x)=\xi$. Then $g(v)\neq0$, as required.
*Case 2: x is algebraic over A.* Then $v^{-1}\in Frac\,A$ is also algebraic over $A$, because $v$ is a polynomial in $x$. hence we have equations of the form
$$
\begin{align}
\tag{3} a_0x^m+a_1x^{m-1}+\dots+a_m&=0\quad(a_i\in A)\\
\tag{4} a_0'v^{-n}+a_1'v^{1-n}+\dots+a_n'&=0\quad(a_j'\in A)
\end{align}
$$
Let $u=a_0a_0'$, and let $f:A\rightarrow \Omega$ be such that $f(u)\neq0$. Then $f$ can be extended, first to a homomorphism $f_1:A[u^{-1}]\rightarrow\Omega$ (with $f_1(u^{-1})=f(u)^{-1}$, this is well-defined by the universal property of $A[u^{-1}]$ since $f(u)\neq0\implies f(u)\text{ is a unit in }\Omega$), and then to a homomorphism $h:C\rightarrow\Omega$, where $C$ is a valuation ring containing $A[u^{-1}]$. (A valuation subring of a field together with a homomorphism is maximal with respect to extension of homomorphisms, see Atiyah, 5.21.) From $(3)$, $x$ is integral over $A[u^{-1}]$, hence $x\in C$. (The integral closure of a subring $A$ in a field $k$ $$\bigcap_{A\subset C\subset k\atop C \text{ is a valuation ring}}C$$see Atiyah, 5.22.) So $B\subset C$ and in particular $v\in C$. On the other hand, from $(4)$, $v^{-1}$ is integral over $A[u^{-1}]$, and therefore is also in $C$. Therefore $v$ is a unit in $C$ and hence $h(v)\neq0$. Now take $g$ to be the restriction of $h$ to $B$.$$\tag*{$\square$}$$
**Remark.** This is a nice application of valuation rings on extension of homomorphisms. The idea is as follows: to extend $f$ onto $B=A[x]$, where $x$ is algebraic over $A$, we want to use the property of valuation rings being maximal with respect to extension of homomorphisms. So first include $a_0^{-1}$ by putting $u=something\times a_0$ and extend $f$ onto $A[\frac{1}{u}]$, denote $f_1$, using the condition $f(u)\neq0$. So $x$ is integral over $A[\frac{1}{u}]$ and we could then extend $f_1$ onto some valuation ring containing $A[\frac{1}{u}]$, denote $h$. Now it suffices to make sure that $h(v)\neq0$. To do this, it suffices to make sure that $v$ is a unit in $C$. Copying the previous method which guarantees $x\in C$, we consider a similar equation $(4)$ and add $a_0'$ into $A[\frac{1}{u}]$ by putting $something=a_0'\enspace i.e.\,u=a_0a_0'$.

**Proof.** Take $A=k, B=E, v=1 \text{ and } \Omega=algebraic\enspace closure\text{ of }k$, then the inclusion $k\subset E$ can be extended to $g:E\rightarrow\Omega$. $\Omega$ is a field and $g\neq0$ implies that $g$ is injective. So $E/k$ is an algebraic extension.$$\tag*{$\square$}$$
