This is essentially a solution for Hartshorne, Exercise III.9.5.
#### Definition of Projective Cone
Consider a closed subscheme $X\subset\mathbb{P}^n_T$. For simplicity, we assume that $T=\mathrm{Spec}\,A$. For general $T$, use relative Proj. Denote $S=A[x_0,\dots,x_n]$ and let $I$ be the unique saturated homogeneous ideal defining $X$. The following lemma can be verified easily:
**Lemma.** Let $S=R[x_0,\dots,x_n]$, then for any homogeneous ideal $I\subset S$, $I[y]\subset S[y]$ is saturated.
**Proof.** For any $s(y)=\sum_is_iy^i\in (I[y])^{sat}$, there exists $m$ such that $y^ms(y)=\sum_is_iy^{i+m}\in I[y]$. Therefore $s_i\in I$ and $s(y)\in I[y]$.$$\tag*{$\square$}$$Then we can define $C(X)$ to be generated by the saturated ideal $I[y]\subset S[y]$. Notice that in this definition $I$ need to be saturated!
Let $I$ be the unique saturated ideal of $X$. Denote $S_t=k(t)[x_0,\dots,x_n]$ for any $t\in T=\mathrm{Spec}\,R$. Then $A\rightarrow k(t)$ induces $S\rightarrow S_t$ and $X_t$ is defined by $IS_t\subset S_t$. By the lemma above, the saturated ideal of $C(X)$ is $I[y]$ and hence $C(X)_t$ is defined by $I[y]S[y]_t=IS_t[y]$. On the other hand, let $I_t\subset S_t$ be the saturation of $IS_t\subset S_t$, then it's totally possible to have $IS_t\subsetneq I_t$. Using the lemma again we see that $IS_t[y]$ and $I_t[y]$ are saturated ideals of $S_t[y]$. Here $IS_t[y]\subset S_t[y]$ defines $C(X)_t$ and $I_t[y]\subset S_t[y]$ defines $C(X_t)$. Now it's clear that in general $C(X_t)$ is a closed subscheme of $C(X)_t$, and that $C(X_t)=C(X)_t$ if and only if $IS_t\subset S_t$ is saturated.
#### Projective Cone Fails to Preserve Flatness
We have a very powerful criterion for flatness:
**Proposition.** Suppose $T$ is an integral noetherian scheme and suppose $X$ is a closed subscheme of $\mathbb{P}_T^n$. Then $X$ is flat over $T$ if and only if the Hilbert polynomial $P_{X_t}$ of $X_t\subset\mathbb{P}_{k(t)}^n$ is independent of $t\in T$.
$P_{X_t}(d)=\mathrm{dim}_{k(t)}\,S_{t,d}/I_{t,d}=:h_{X_t}(d)$ and $P_{C(X)_t}(d)=\mathrm{dim}_{k(t)}\,S_t[y]_d/(IS_t[y])_d=\mathrm{dim}_{k(t)}\,(S_t/I_t)[y]=:h_{C(X)_t}(d)$ for all $d\gg0$. Notice that we use saturated ideals for $h$! For small $d$ the choice of ideal makes a difference.$$h_{C(X)_t}(d)=\sum_{l=0}^dh_{X_t}(l)$$So we see that $X\rightarrow T$ is flat doesn't imply that $C(X)\rightarrow T$ is flat, because values of $h_{X_t}$ for small $d$ may be relevant to $t$. The following is a concrete counterexample:
Let $T=\mathbb{A}_k^1$, $X_t=\{[1:0:0],[0:1:0],[1:1:t]\}$ with reduced structure. Then $X$ is the disjoint union of three copies of $T$. Clearly $X$ is flat over $T$. Some simple linear algebra implies that$$h_{X_t}(d)=\mathrm{dim}_{k(t)}\{d\text{ forms vanishing on three points}\}=\left\{
\begin{align}
2,\quad &t=0\text{ and }d=1;\\
3,\quad &\text{otherwise}.
\end{align}
\right.
$$
#### Very Flat Family
Above calculations motivate the following notion:
Let $X\subset\mathbb{P}_T^n$ be a closed subscheme, where $T$ is a noetherian integral scheme. For each $t\in T$, let $I_t\subset S_t=k(t)[x_0,\dots,x_n]$ be the unique saturated ideal of $X_t$ in $\mathbb{P}_{k(t)}^n$. We say that the family $\{X_t\}$ is very flat if for all $d\geq0$, $\mathrm{dim}_{k(t)}\,(S_t/I_t)_d$ is independent of $t$.
If $\{X_t\}$ is a very flat family in $\mathbb{P}^n$, then it is clearly flat and also that $\{C(X_t)\}$ is a very flat family in $\mathbb{P}^{n+1}$, and hence flat. Next we prove the following sufficient condition for very flatness:
**Theorem.** If $\{X_{(t)}\}$ is an algebraic family of projectively normal varieties in $\mathbb{P}_k^n$, parametrized by a nonsingular curve $T$ over an algebraically closed field $k$, then $\{X_{(t)}\}$ is a very flat family of schemes.

**Proof.** WLOG, we may assume that $T=\mathrm{Spec}\,R$ where $R$ is a DVR. Denote $0$ the generic point and $1$ the closed point. Omit $X$ for $h_{X_t}$ and denote $r_t(d)=\mathrm{dim}_{k(t)}\,I_{t,d}$. Then $r_t(d)+h_t(d)=\binom{n+1}{d}$. Our goal is to show that for any $d\geq0$ $h_0(d)=h_1(d)$, or equivalently $r_0(d)=r_1(d)$.
Notice that since $R$ is a PID, $I$ is flat over $I$ if and only if it's torsion-free. But $I$ is an ideal of an integral domain, so $I$ is necessarily flat and hence the ideal sheaf $\tilde{I}$ of $X$ is flat over $T$. In particular, since $R$ is local, $I_d$ is a free $R$-module for all $d\geq0$.
**Lemma.** $I_0=IS_0=I\otimes_Rk(0)$. Equivalently in terms of sheaves, since $\tilde{I}_0$ is the ideal sheaf of $X_0$, $I_{0,d}=H^0(\mathbb{P}_{k(0)}^n,\tilde{I}_0(d))=(IS_0)_d$ and hence $r_0(d)=\mathrm{rank}_RI_d=\mathrm{dim}_{k(0)}\,H^0(\mathbb{P}_{k(0)}^n,\tilde{I}_0(d))$.
**Proof.** For any $s\in S_0$ such that for each $i=0,\dots,n$ there is an $n$ such that $x_i^ns\in IS_0$. There exists $a\in I$ and $b\in R-{0}$ such that $x_i^ns=a/b$. Then $x_i^nbs=a\in I$. Since $I$ is saturated, we have $bs\in I$. Therefore $s\in IS_0$ and $IS_0$ is saturated. Then $I_0=IS_0$ by definition.$$\tag*{$\square$}$$==Caution!== Let $I$ be a homogeneous ideal of $S=A[x_0,\dots,x_n]$, then $X=\mathrm{Proj}\,S/I$ is integral if and only if ==the saturation of== $I$ is prime. In general, let $T$ be a graded ring, then $\mathrm{Proj}\,T$ is integral do NOT imply that $T$ is an integral domain.

Now at the closed point, since $X$ is a variety and $I$ is saturated, $S/I$ is an integral domain and hence $\mathrm{Tor}_1^R(S/I,k(1))=0$. So $I\otimes_Rk(1)=\mathrm{im}\,(I\otimes_Rk(1)\rightarrow S\otimes_Rk(1))=\mathrm{im}\,(I\otimes_SS_1\rightarrow S_1)=IS_1$. In terms of sheaves, $\tilde{I}_1$ is again the ideal sheaf of $X_1$. So we also have $r_1(d)=\mathrm{dim}_{k(1)}\,H^0(\mathbb{P}^n_{k(1)},\tilde{I}_1(d))$. However, unlike the generic point, here it's possible to have $IS_1\subsetneq I_1$ in general. So far we only know that$$r_0(d)=\mathrm{rank}_RI_d=\mathrm{dim}_{k(1)}\,(IS_1)_d\leq\mathrm{dim}_{k(1)}\,I_{1,d}=r_1(d).$$Hartshorne Ex.II.5.14.(d) tells that $X_1$ is projectively normal if and only if it is normal and for every $d\geq0$, $\Gamma(\mathbb{P}_{k(1)}^n,\mathcal{O}(d))\rightarrow\Gamma(X_1,\mathcal{O}(d))$ is surjective. So we have an exact sequence$$0\longrightarrow H^0(\mathbb{P}^n_{k(1)},\tilde{I}_1(d))\longrightarrow H^0(\mathbb{P}_{k(1)}^n,\mathcal{O}(d))\longrightarrow H^0(X_1,\mathcal{O}(d))\longrightarrow0,$$which implies that $h_1(d)=\mathrm{dim}_{k(1)}\,H^0(X_1,\mathcal{O}(d))$.
Hartshorne, III.9.11 then tells that $X\rightarrow T$ is flat. Then by semicontinuous theorem we see that $\mathrm{dim}_{k(0)}\,H^0(X_0,\mathcal{O}(d))\leq h_1(d)$. On the other hand, we also have an exact sequence$$0\longrightarrow H^0(\mathbb{P}^n_{k(0)},\tilde{I}_0(d))\longrightarrow H^0(\mathbb{P}_{k(0)}^n,\mathcal{O}(d))\longrightarrow H^0(X_0,\mathcal{O}(d)),$$which implies that $\binom{n+1}{d}-r_0(d)\leq\mathrm{dim}_{k(0)}\,H^0(X_0,\mathcal{O}(d))$. Therefore $$r_1(d)=\binom{n+1}{d}-h_1(d)\leq\binom{n+1}{d}-\mathrm{dim}_{k(0)}\,H^0(X_0,\mathcal{O}(d))\leq r_0(d).$$And hence $r_0(d)=r_1(d)$.$$\tag*{$\square$}$$**Remark.** This has a biproduct that $IS_1$ is saturated and hence $C(X_t)=C(X)_t$ for all $t\in T$. In fact, we have seen that very flatness is equivalent to the fact that $IS_1$ is saturated. Since $IS_1$ is saturated if and only if $\mathrm{depth}_{S_{1,+}}S_1/IS_1\geq1$ and the condition says that $S_1/I_1$ is an normal domain, I believe that a proof can be found by relating the depth of $S_1/I_1$ with $S_1/IS_1$, where $I_1$ is the saturation of $IS_1$. However I haven't investigated into details.
