**Fiber product is the standard tool dealing with the situation of two objects mapping to one.**
e.g. Let $X$ be a nonsingular projective curve, and $\mathcal{F}\in\mathcal{Coh_X}$. By Serre's theorem (Hartshorne I.5.18) we know that there's a locally free sheaf $\mathcal{E}_0=\mathcal{O}(-n)^m$ with a surjective map $\mathcal{E}_0\rightarrow\mathcal{F}$. Since local rings of $X$ are DVR and hence PID, and submodules of a free module over a PID are free, the kernel is locally free. So we obtain a locally free resolution of $\mathcal{F}$:$$0\longrightarrow\mathcal{E}_1\longrightarrow\mathcal{E}_0\longrightarrow\mathcal{F}\longrightarrow0$$Define $\det\mathcal{F}:=\det\mathcal{E}_0\otimes(\det\mathcal{E}_1)^{-1}$. (Notice that this definition is consistent if $\mathcal{F}$ is locally free.) We shall show that this definition is independent the choice of $\mathcal{E}_1$ and $\mathcal{E}_0$.
Suppose we have another resolution$$0\longrightarrow\mathcal{L}_1\longrightarrow\mathcal{L}_0\longrightarrow\mathcal{F}\longrightarrow0$$We need to show that $\det\mathcal{E}_0\otimes\det\mathcal{L}_1=\det\mathcal{L}_0\otimes\det\mathcal{E}_1$. Now to deal with two resolutions of one object, we introduce the fiber product sheaf$$\mathcal{M}=\mathcal{E}_0\times_{\mathcal{F}}\mathcal{L}_0:U\longmapsto\{(e,l)\in\Gamma(U,\mathcal{E}_0\oplus\mathcal{L}_0):\pi_{\mathcal{E}}(e)=\pi_{\mathcal{L}}(l)\in\Gamma(U,\mathcal{F})\}$$Verify that $\mathcal{M}$ is locally free and we get two exact sequences:$$\begin{align}0\longrightarrow\mathcal{L}_1\longrightarrow\mathcal{M}\longrightarrow\mathcal{E}_0\longrightarrow0\\
0\longrightarrow\mathcal{E}_1\longrightarrow\mathcal{M}\longrightarrow\mathcal{L}_0\longrightarrow0\end{align}$$So $\det\mathcal{M}=\det\mathcal{E}_0\otimes(\det\mathcal{L}_1)^{-1}=\det\mathcal{L}_0\otimes(\det\mathcal{E}_1)^{-1}$ as desired.
**Remark.** This is the idea of Schanuel's lemma. And we actually have$$0\longrightarrow\mathcal{M}\longrightarrow\mathcal{E_0}\oplus\mathcal{L}_0\xrightarrow{\pi_{\mathcal{E}}-\pi_{\mathcal{L}}}\mathcal{F}\longrightarrow0$$Notice how the appropriate object is carved out from the basic attempt by kernel!

Here's another example from Hartshorne Ex. III.3.2. Let $X$ be a reduced noetherian scheme. Show that $X$ is affine if and only if each irreducible component is affine.
This could be prove by applying the Chevalley's theorem to the finite morphism $\coprod_i X_i\rightarrow X$, where $X_i$'s are the irreducible components of $X$. But we have a constructive proof as follows.
We proceed by induction on the number of distinct irreducible components. When there's only one, there's nothing to prove. Now suppose there're $n$ components. Let $Y=X_1\cup\cdots\cup X_{n-1}$ and $Z=X_n$. By induction hypothesis $Y$ is affine. Suppose $Y=\mathrm{Spec}\,A$ and $Z=\mathrm{Spec}\,B$. Let $W=Y\cap Z$. Then $W$ is a closed subscheme of an affine scheme and hence $W$ is affine. Let $W=\mathrm{Spec}\,C$ and corresponding to the closed immersions we have two homomorphisms $\varphi:A\rightarrow C$ and $\psi:B\rightarrow C$. Following the slogan, we construct the fiber product ring$$D=A\times_C B=\{(a,b)\in A\times B\,:\,\varphi(a)=\psi(b)\}.$$This is just the algebraic formulation of patching two schemes along one common subscheme. We could prove that $X=\mathrm{Spec}\,D$.
The existence of $D$ establishes a special result that we can attach two affine schemes along a common closed subscheme to get a another affine scheme. Notice that for general schemes the analogy statement is much harder.
